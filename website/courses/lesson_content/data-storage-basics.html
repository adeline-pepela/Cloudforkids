Topic 1: What Is Data? Different Types of Information We Store

Data is simply information. Every piece of information on your device is data: photos, videos, text messages, emails, homework documents, app preferences, music, passwords, and more! Different types of data take different amounts of storage space. A text message (maybe 1 kilobyte or KB) takes much less space than a YouTube video (maybe 50-500 megabytes or MB). Understanding data types helps you manage your device's storage. When you see a notification "Your phone storage is full," it means you have too much data stored and need to delete some or move it to the cloud. In Kenya, many students take photos and videos on their phones. These media files are the largest data types and fill up storage quickly. Backing up photos to Google Photos (cloud storage) frees up space on your phone. Data types include: Text data (documents, messages), Image data (photos), Video data (movies, TikTok videos), Audio data (music, voice messages), Program data (apps themselves), System data (settings, contacts). Every app you use stores data: WhatsApp stores messages and media, YouTube remembers your watch history, Instagram stores photos and comments. Understanding what data exists helps you control your digital footprint and privacy.

Key Takeaways
  • Data is information stored on devices: photos, videos, messages, documents, and more
  • Different data types use different amounts of storage: Text uses least space, videos use most
  • File sizes measured in: Bytes (B), Kilobytes (KB), Megabytes (MB), Gigabytes (GB), Terabytes (TB)
  • 1 KB = 1,000 bytes; 1 MB = 1,000 KB; 1 GB = 1,000 MB; 1 TB = 1,000 GB
  • A text message: ~1 KB; A photo: ~3-5 MB; A video: ~50-500 MB depending on quality and length
  • When storage is full, you can delete data or move it to cloud storage or external devices
  • Apps store data about you: messages, watch history, preferences, locations, and contacts
  • Understanding what data you have helps you protect privacy and manage storage

Topic 2: Local, External and Cloud Storage - Three Places to Keep Your Data

Data has to live somewhere, and there are three main places it can be kept. The first is LOCAL STORAGE: the memory built inside your phone, tablet or computer. Your phone's photo gallery, downloaded songs and installed apps all sit in local storage. It is fast and works without internet, but if the device is lost, stolen or broken, the data inside it can be lost too. The second is EXTERNAL STORAGE: removable devices such as flash disks (USB drives), memory cards (SD cards) and external hard drives. Many students in Kenya carry a flash disk to the cyber café to print homework, and many phones have a memory card slot for extra photos. External storage is easy to carry and works without internet, but small devices are easy to lose and can catch viruses when plugged into many computers. The third is CLOUD STORAGE: space on servers in data centres that you reach through the internet, such as Google Drive, Google Photos, OneDrive, iCloud and Dropbox. Cloud storage keeps your files safe even if your phone breaks, and lets you open them from any device, but you need internet (and bundles) to upload and download, and you must protect your account with a strong password. Smart people do not choose just one - they use all three together, keeping important files in more than one place.

Key Takeaways
  • Local storage is the memory inside your own device - fast and works offline, but lost if the device is lost or broken
  • External storage is removable: flash disks (USB drives), SD memory cards and external hard drives
  • Cloud storage keeps your files on servers in data centres that you reach through the internet (Google Drive, OneDrive, iCloud, Dropbox)
  • Cloud storage lets you open your files from any device, but you need internet and a strong password
  • Flash disks shared at cyber cafés can spread viruses - scan them before opening files
  • The safest plan uses more than one type of storage for important files

Topic 3: Storage Capacity - Bits, Bytes, MB, GB and TB

Every storage device can only hold a certain amount of data. This amount is called its CAPACITY. Computers store everything as tiny switches that are either ON or OFF, written as 1 or 0. One of these switches is called a BIT. Eight bits make one BYTE, which is about enough to store a single letter like "A". Because data gets very big very quickly, we use bigger units, just like we use grams and kilograms. About 1,000 bytes make a KILOBYTE (KB) - a short SMS. About 1,000 kilobytes make a MEGABYTE (MB) - one photo or one song is a few megabytes. About 1,000 megabytes make a GIGABYTE (GB) - a full-length movie can be 1 to 2 GB. About 1,000 gigabytes make a TERABYTE (TB) - enough for hundreds of thousands of photos. (Computers often count in 1,024s instead of 1,000s, which is why a "64 GB" phone shows a little less free space.) In Kenya, you see these units every day: data bundles are sold in MB and GB, and phones are sold as 32 GB, 64 GB or 128 GB. Knowing the units helps you make smart choices - for example, streaming one hour of video can use hundreds of megabytes, so it can finish a small bundle quickly, while sending a WhatsApp text uses almost nothing.

Key Takeaways
  • Capacity is how much data a storage device can hold
  • A bit is a single 1 or 0; 8 bits make 1 byte (about one letter)
  • The units grow in steps of about 1,000: KB (kilobyte) then MB (megabyte) then GB (gigabyte) then TB (terabyte)
  • A text message is about 1 KB, a photo is a few MB, a movie can be 1-2 GB
  • Phones are sold by capacity (32 GB, 64 GB, 128 GB) and data bundles are sold in MB and GB
  • Videos use the most space and the most bundles - check sizes before you download or stream

Topic 4: Keeping Data Safe and Organised - Backups, Files, Folders and Databases

Having lots of storage is not enough - data must also be SAFE and EASY TO FIND. To keep data safe, we use REDUNDANCY: keeping more than one copy of important data in different places. A simple rule many professionals use is the 3-2-1 rule: keep 3 copies of important data, on 2 different types of storage (for example your phone and a flash disk), with 1 copy somewhere else (for example the cloud). Cloud companies do this too - when you save a photo, their data centres quietly keep several copies on different machines so nothing is lost if one breaks. Old files you rarely need can be moved to an ARCHIVE, a long-term store that keeps them safe without filling up your phone. To keep data easy to find, computers use a FILE SYSTEM: files (single items like "Science_Homework.docx") are kept inside folders (like "Grade 5 > Science"), just like papers in labelled files in a cabinet. Good file names with dates and subjects make searching easy. When there is a huge amount of information, organisations use a DATABASE: an organised collection of data in tables of rows and columns that a computer can search in a split second. M-Pesa uses databases to look up your balance among millions of customers, KNEC uses databases to store exam results, and your school may keep learner records in one. A database is like a super-fast, super-organised register book.

Key Takeaways
  • Redundancy means keeping more than one copy of important data in different places
  • The 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 types of storage, with 1 copy somewhere else (like the cloud)
  • An archive is long-term storage for old files you rarely open
  • A file system organises data into files inside folders, like papers in a filing cabinet
  • Clear file names (subject, topic, date) make files easy to find
  • A database stores data in organised tables so computers can search millions of records in a split second
  • M-Pesa, exam results systems and school records all use databases

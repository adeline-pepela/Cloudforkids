A router connects many devices into one network - the internet connects networks together.

🎯 What You'll Learn
  • Explain what a network is using a real-world example
  • Describe how the internet connects millions of smaller networks together
🧠 The Big Idea

A network is just a group of devices that can talk to each other - like a classroom where everyone can pass notes. A router is the helper in the middle that makes sure each note goes to the right person. The internet is the biggest network of all: millions of smaller networks (homes, schools, businesses) all connected together, so a message from Nairobi can reach a server in another country in a fraction of a second.

💡

Did You Know? A message can hop through a dozen different routers in well under a second to cross from one side of the world to the other.

🛠️ Try It Yourself

Build a 'Paper Network': draw 5-6 houses on paper, each representing a device, and place one 'router' card in the middle. Connect every house to the router with string or yarn. Write a short message on a slip of paper and pass it from one house to the router, and have the router hand it to the right house. Then try connecting two separate paper networks with a single string - that's like two schools joining the internet.

🌍

Real-World Connection: Kenya's National Fibre Optic Backbone links towns and cities the same way your paper network links houses to a router - smaller networks joined into one big network that connects to the rest of the world.

🔍

Dig Deeper: Every network, no matter how small, follows the same basic rules as the Paper Network you built. Real routers use a kind of digital address book to decide, in a fraction of a millisecond, exactly which cable or wireless signal to send a message down next. When your message needs to leave your home network and travel across the country - or across the world - it hops between dozens of routers run by different companies and countries, each one just passing it a little further along, like a very fast game of relay. Engineers call this 'routing', and it's happening billions of times a second, all over the planet, right now.

✅ Quick Check

Why does a message need a router instead of just being shouted to every device at once?

Reveal a model answer

If every device shouted at once, messages would get lost or jumbled. A router checks each message's address and sends it only where it needs to go.
